    What the model assumes

    The kinetic theory assumptions for an ideal gas (9476 3(a)). This is an ideal-gas simulation, so every one holds exactly here. Real gases break the second and third most: see Real vs ideal.

    • Particles are in rapid, random motion.Each particle moves in a straight line until it hits a wall or another particle, in no preferred direction.
    • The volume of the particles is negligible compared with the volume of the container.The particles are tiny dots in a large box.
    • There are no intermolecular forces between the particles.Between collisions no particle attracts or repels any other.
    • Collisions are perfectly elastic.No kinetic energy is lost when particles hit each other or the walls.
    • The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the absolute temperature.Doubling T (in K) doubles the average kinetic energy; speeds rise by √2.

    Real vs ideal

    For 1 mol of an ideal gas pV/RT = 1 at every pressure. For a real gas it is not. The curves come from the van der Waals model, which is qualitatively right but is a model, not measured data.

    Why the deviations grow

    • At very high pressure the particles are pushed close together. Their own volume is no longer negligible compared with the volume of the container, so the gas takes up more space than an ideal gas would: pV/RT > 1.
    • At very low temperature the particles move more slowly. Their kinetic energy is no longer large compared with the intermolecular forces of attraction, which pull on a particle as it approaches a wall. Collisions with the walls are less frequent and less forceful, so p is lower than ideal: pV/RT < 1. Cool it enough and the gas condenses.
    • At moderate pressures attractions usually win first (the dip below 1). At higher pressures particle volume takes over (the rise above 1).
    • So a gas approaches ideal behaviour at low pressure and high temperature: particles far apart and moving fast.

    Which gases are most nearly ideal

    • He and H2 are closest to ideal. They have very few electrons, so small electron clouds that are hard to polarise and only very weak id-id attractions; the particles are also small. At 300 K their attractions are so weak that particle volume wins at every pressure.
    • NH3 deviates most at moderate pressure: its molecules are polar and hydrogen bond to one another (on top of pd-pd and id-id), the strongest attractions here.
    • CO2 is non-polar, but it has a large electron cloud (22 electrons), so its id-id attractions are strong and the dip below 1 is deep.
    • N2 and CH4 lie in between.

    EnrichmentThe van der Waals equation

    (p + a/Vm2)(Vm − b) = RT

    Vm is the molar volume. The term a/Vm2 corrects for intermolecular attractions and b for the volume of the particles themselves. The page solves this equation for Vm (gas root) at each point and plots pVm/RT. Below a gas's critical temperature, Tc, pressure alone can liquefy it; the gas curve then stops at the pressure where the model predicts condensation (equal-area rule) instead of showing the liquid.

    The van der Waals model is simple: it gets the shape right but overestimates condensation pressures (for example NH3 at 300 K: about 3.0 MPa in the model, about 1.1 MPa in reality). The figures in the notes (Part 2) use a more accurate equation of state, so values differ slightly.

    Literature constants. R = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1 = 0.0831 dm3 bar K−1 mol−1.

    Dalton's Law of partial pressures

    In a mixture of gases that do not react, each gas exerts the pressure it would exert if it alone filled the container, pi = niRT/V. The total pressure is the sum: ptotal = p1 + p2 + … Dividing, pi/ptotal = ni/ntotal = xi, the mole fraction, so pi = xi × ptotal.
