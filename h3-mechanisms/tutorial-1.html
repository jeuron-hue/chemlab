H3.6.T1 §1

Question 1

In the radical substitution of an alkane, the step that decides which H atom is replaced is X• + R–H → H–X + R•.

  1. Using the Data Booklet bond energies C–H 410, H–Cl 431 and H–Br 366 kJ mol−1, calculate ΔH of this step for X = Cl and for X = Br. [2]
  2. Use the Hammond postulate to describe the transition state of each step. [2]
  3. Propane with Cl2 in light at 25 °C gives 45 % 1-chloropropane and 55 % 2-chloropropane; with Br2 at 125 °C it gives 3 % 1-bromopropane and 97 % 2-bromopropane. A secondary radical is more stable than a primary radical. Calculate how many times more reactive one secondary H atom is than one primary H atom in each case, and explain why bromination is far more selective. [3]

  1. Identify the rate-determining step and write the rate equation. [2]
  2. Use the Hammond postulate to say which species the first transition state resembles, and draw it. [2]
  3. Explain, using the steady-state approximation, why the concentration of the intermediate stays very small, and why [OH−] does not appear in the rate equation. [2]

Question 5

  1. The relative rates of SN2 reaction of 1-bromopropane with three nucleophiles under the same conditions are: H2O 1; CH3COO− 500; OH− 1.6 × 104. Explain this order. (pKa of CH3COOH 4.74; pKa of H2O 15.7: literature values, Topic 10.) [3]

Question 6

  1. The relative rates of SN2 reaction with NaI in propanone are: 1-bromopentane 1.0; 1-bromo-3-methylbutane 0.9; 1-bromo-2-methylbutane 0.03; 2-bromopentane 0.02. Explain the effect of each change of structure, compared with 1-bromopentane. [4]
  2. For SN1 hydrolysis, put 1-bromopentane, 2-bromopentane and 2-bromo-2-methylpentane in order of increasing rate. For each, draw the carbocation formed and state how many C–H bonds can take part in hyperconjugation. [4]

H3.6.T1 §7

Question 7

This question is based on a classic experiment of 1935. (S)-2-Iodooctane, CH3CHI(CH2)5CH3, is dissolved in propanone with Na128I (iodide containing radioactive iodine-128). Two rates are followed: the rate at which 128I enters the organic compound, and the rate at which the optical rotation of the solution falls. Both are first order with respect to 2-iodooctane and first order with respect to iodide, with rate constants k(exchange) = 1.5 × 10−5 and k(loss of rotation) = 3.0 × 10−5 mol−1 dm3 s−1.

  1. Name the mechanism of the exchange, and explain how the rate equation supports it. [2]
  2. Draw the transition state of the exchange. [2]
  3. Explain why the rotation falls twice as fast as 128I is taken up. [3]
  4. State the configuration of the 2-iodooctane formed in one exchange. [1]

  1. Explain why the product is neither a single enantiomer nor a racemic mixture. [3]
  2. Give the configuration of the enantiomer in excess, and calculate the percentage of each enantiomer in the product. [2]
  3. Predict, with a reason, how the enantiomeric excess would change if the carbocation were more stable and so lived longer before it was attacked. [2]

H3.6.T1 §10

Question 10

A chemist needs (S)-2-methylpentanenitrile, CH3CH2CH2CH(CH3)CN, and starts from (R)-pentan-2-ol (ee 99 %).

  • Route 1: conc. HBr, heat → 2-bromopentane; then KCN in ethanol, heat. The nitrile is mainly the (R) enantiomer, with an ee of only 38 %.
  • Route 2: CH3SO2Cl, (C2H5)3N, 0 °C → the methanesulfonate (mesylate) ester; then NaCN, 40 °C. The nitrile has an ee of 98 %.
  1. Why can the alcohol not be converted directly into the nitrile with CN−? [1]
  2. Explain why the mesylate has the same configuration as the alcohol. [1]
  3. Give the mechanism of the second step of route 2, and explain why the nitrile is (S). [2]
  4. Suggest why route 1 gives mainly the (R)-nitrile, and why its ee is much lower. [3]
  5. Suggest why the final product is tested for traces of the mesylate. [1]
