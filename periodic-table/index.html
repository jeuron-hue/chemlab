Electronegativity difference, ΔEN (Pauling) literature
The larger ΔEN, the more ionic the bonding. AlCl3 is the exception named in the syllabus: Al is a metal, and metal chlorides such as NaCl and MgCl2 are ionic, so you might expect AlCl3 to be ionic too. It isn't: the small, highly charged Al3+ polarises the large Cl− so strongly that the bonding is covalent.
Al2Cl6 dimer in the vapour at the sublimation temperature
Each bridging Cl uses a lone pair to form a dative (co-ordinate) bond, shown as an arrow, to the other Al. Each Al then has four bonding pairs (an octet) and is tetrahedral.

Bench · 9476 5(f)–(g)

Groups 2 and 17

Reducing and oxidising power from E⦵ values, halogen displacement, thermal stability of Group 2 carbonates and of the hydrogen halides.

The more negative E⦵(M2+/M), the greater the tendency of M to lose electrons: the stronger the reducing agent.

The more positive E⦵(X2/X−), the greater the tendency of X2 to gain electrons: the stronger the oxidising agent.

Less energy is needed to form M2+ down the group: the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and better shielded. So reactivity as reducing agents increases from Mg to Ba.
