  2. Two 2px orbitals, one on each atom. [3]

Question 3 (Further example)

  1. Draw the MO diagram of each ion. [2]
  2. Calculate the bond order of each ion. [2]
  3. State and explain whether each ion is paramagnetic or diamagnetic. [2]

H3.1.T1 §6

Question 6 (Further example)

Read the passage and answer the questions that follow.

Four species contain two bonded oxygen atoms. The dioxygenyl cation O2+ was first made by Neil Bartlett in 1962, in the solid O2[PtF6]. Ordinary O2 makes up about a fifth of air. The superoxide ion O2− is found in potassium superoxide, KO2, a solid used in rebreathers and spacecraft to remove exhaled carbon dioxide and replace it with oxygen:

4KO2(s) + 2CO2(g) → 2K2CO3(s) + 3O2(g)

The peroxide ion O22− is found in sodium peroxide, Na2O2. The O–O bond lengths are O2+ 0.112 nm, O2 0.121 nm, O2− 0.128 nm and O22− 0.149 nm (literature values). KO2 is paramagnetic; Na2O2 is diamagnetic.

  1. Write the valence MO configuration of O2− and calculate its bond order. [2]
  2. Explain the trend in the O–O bond lengths. [2]
  3. Explain the magnetic behaviour of KO2 and Na2O2. [2]
  4. Find the oxidation number of oxygen in KO2 and in Na2O2. [2]
  5. Use oxidation numbers to show that the rebreather reaction is a disproportionation of oxygen. [2]
  6. The Data Booklet gives the bond energies of O=O (496 kJ mol−1) and O–O (150 kJ mol−1). Use your answer to (a) to estimate the O–O bond energy in O2−, explaining your reasoning. Assume the bond energy changes smoothly with bond order. [2]

  1. Identify the HOMO and the LUMO of H2 and of Ne2 (use the valence MOs of Part 1). [2]
  2. An F2 molecule accepts one electron. State which MO receives it, and what happens to the bond order and the strength of the bond. [2]
  3. An O2 molecule accepts one electron. State which MO receives it, and explain why. [2]

H3.1.T1 §8

Question 8 (Further example)

The Data Booklet gives these bond energies: Cl–Cl 244 kJ mol−1, F–F 158 kJ mol−1 and O–O 150 kJ mol−1 (the O–O single bond, as in peroxides).

  1. State the bond order of F2. Then, using the valence MO order of Part 1 with 3s and 3p in place of 2s and 2p, state the bond order of Cl2. [1]
  2. Chlorine atoms are larger than fluorine atoms. Explain why the F–F bond is nevertheless weaker than the Cl–Cl bond. [3]
  3. A student says that the stronger a bond is, the higher its bond order must be. Use the bond energies of O–O and Cl–Cl to show that bond energy alone does not tell you the bond order. [2]

H3.1.T1 §9

Question 9 (Further example)

By analogy with HF in Part 1, describe the bonding in HCl in MO terms. Take the H–Cl bond as the z axis, and use the 1s orbital of H and the 3s and 3p orbitals of Cl.

  1. State which atomic orbital of Cl combines with H 1s, and name the MOs formed. [2]
  2. State which atomic orbitals of Cl are non-bonding, and explain why. [2]
  3. Draw the MO diagram, and state the number of lone pairs and the bond order. [3]

H3.1.T1 §10

Question 10 (Further example)

A paramagnetic oxygen analyser is used on the headspace of a reactor. The headspace may contain H2, He, CH4, CO, O2, NO and NO2. The analyser responds to any gas that is drawn into a magnetic field.

  1. Which of these gases are paramagnetic? Explain your answer for each paramagnetic gas. [3]
  2. Explain why H2, He, CH4 and CO do not interfere with the reading. [1]
  3. Explain why the Lewis structure of O2 would not have predicted that the analyser works. [2]
