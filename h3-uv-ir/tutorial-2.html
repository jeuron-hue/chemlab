H3.2.T2 §1

Question 1

The diatomic gases carbon monoxide, nitrogen monoxide, hydrogen bromide and chlorine are found in some industrial waste gases. CO absorbs at 2143 cm−1, NO at 1876 cm−1 and HBr at 2559 cm−1 (literature values).

  1. State which of CO, NO, HBr and Cl2 absorb infrared radiation, and explain your answer using the dipole rule. [3]
  2. Each of these molecules has only one vibration. Suggest two reasons why an IR-active diatomic gas is likely to be a much weaker greenhouse gas, molecule for molecule, than a polyatomic gas such as CO2. [2]

H3.2.T2 §2

Question 2

Carbon disulfide, CS2, is a linear molecule, S=C=S.

  1. State the number of vibrations of CS2. [1]
  2. Draw diagrams to show each vibration, using arrows to show how the atoms move. [3]
  3. State which vibrations are IR active, and the number of absorptions in the IR spectrum of CS2. [2]
  4. The IR spectrum of CS2 vapour shows absorptions at 1535 and 397 cm−1 (literature values). Assign each to a vibration, and explain why the symmetric stretch, at about 658 cm−1, does not appear. [2]

H3.2.T2 §3

Question 3

Carbonyl sulfide, OCS, is linear, O=C=S. Its IR spectrum shows absorptions at 2062, 859 and 520 cm−1 (literature values).

  1. State the number of vibrations of OCS. [1]
  2. Assign each absorption to a vibration. [2]
  3. Explain why OCS shows one more absorption than CO2, although both are linear triatomic molecules. [2]

H3.2.T2 §4

Question 4

Hydrogen sulfide, H2S, absorbs at 2626, 2615 and 1183 cm−1; water absorbs at 3756, 3657 and 1595 cm−1 (gas, literature values).

  1. State the shape of H2S, its number of vibrations and the number of IR absorptions expected. [2]
  2. Assign the three absorptions of H2S to vibrations. [1]
  3. Suggest why each absorption of H2S is at a lower wavenumber than the matching absorption of water. [2]

H3.2.T2 §5

Question 5

C≡C bonds absorb at 2150–2250 cm−1 and C=C bonds of alkenes at 1635–1690 cm−1 (Data Booklet); C–C single bonds absorb at about 800–1200 cm−1 (literature value). The Data Booklet bond energies are C≡C 840, C=C 610 and C–C 350 kJ mol−1.

  1. Explain the trend in wavenumber in terms of the bonds. [3]
  2. The bond energies of C–H (410 kJ mol−1) and C–Cl (340 kJ mol−1) are not very different, yet C–H absorbs at 2850–2950 cm−1 and C–Cl at 700–800 cm−1. Explain this difference. [2]

H3.2.T2 §6

Question 6

Butanal, butanone and but-3-en-1-ol, CH2=CH–CH2–CH2OH, are isomers of formula C4H8O. Fig 1 shows the simulated IR spectra A, B and C of the three compounds, in an unknown order.

  1. Identify which spectrum belongs to but-3-en-1-ol. Explain your answer by identifying three absorptions. [3]
  2. Identify the functional group responsible for the strongest absorption in B and in C, and explain why the Data Booklet table does not allow you to decide which of B and C is butanal. [2]
  3. Describe a chemical test that would distinguish butanal from butanone, stating what you would see with each. [2]

H3.2.T2 §7

Question 7

Butan-1-ol is oxidised with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) (11.6). In experiment 1 the product D is distilled off as it forms; in experiment 2 the mixture is heated under reflux and gives product E. Their IR spectra show:

  • D: absorptions at 2950 and 2870 cm−1, and a strong absorption at 1730 cm−1; no broad absorption above 3000 cm−1.
  • E: a strong, very broad absorption from about 2500 to 3300 cm−1, and strong absorptions at 1710 and 1280 cm−1.
  1. Identify D and E, explaining how the absorptions support your answers. [4]
  2. Explain which feature of the spectrum of D shows that no butan-1-ol remains in it. [2]

H3.2.T2 §8

Question 8

Paracetamol is N-(4-hydroxyphenyl)ethanamide, HO–C6H4–NH–CO–CH3 (the two groups on the benzene ring are opposite each other).

  1. Use the Data Booklet to predict the characteristic absorptions in the IR spectrum of paracetamol. For each, give the bond, the range and the expected appearance. [4]
  2. Identify two of these absorptions that are likely to overlap. [2]

H3.2.T2 §9

Question 9

A liquid of formula C3H6O2 has strong IR absorptions at 1735, 1180 and 1060 cm−1, a C–H absorption near 2950 cm−1, and no broad absorption anywhere above 2500 cm−1.

  1. Identify the functional group most likely to be present, and explain why the compound is not propanoic acid. [3]
  2. Give the structural formulae of two esters of formula C3H6O2 that fit the spectrum. [2]
  3. Suggest a method that could decide between these two esters. [1]

H3.2.T2 §10

Question 10

Read the passage and answer the questions that follow.

Sulfur hexafluoride, SF6, is an octahedral molecule used as an insulating gas in high-voltage electrical switchgear. Its strongest IR absorption is at 948 cm−1, from an S–F stretching vibration. It has one of the highest global warming potentials of the gases assessed by the IPCC: its 100-year GWP is 23 500, and it stays in the atmosphere for about 3200 years. Its concentration in the atmosphere is about 10 parts per trillion, while that of carbon dioxide is above 420 parts per million (literature values).

  1. Convert 948 cm−1 to a wavelength in µm, and state its significance for the greenhouse effect. [2]
  2. SF6 has no dipole moment. Explain why it can still absorb infrared radiation. [2]
  3. Explain why SF6 has such a large global warming potential. [3]
  4. Explain why carbon dioxide nevertheless contributes far more to global warming than SF6. [2]
  5. SF6 has 15 vibrations. Suggest why its IR spectrum shows far fewer than 15 absorptions. [1]
