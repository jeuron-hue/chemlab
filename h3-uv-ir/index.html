Beer–Lambert calibration bench

Measure iron in water samples as the orange-red complex [Fe(phen)3]2+: zero with a reagent blank, measure the standards, draw the calibration graph and read off the unknowns. The Beer–Lambert law, A = lg(I0/I) = εcl, makes absorbance proportional to concentration, so the calibration graph is a straight line through the origin with gradient εl. The spectrometer, the samples and their readings are simulated (illustrative data); ε = 1.11 × 104 dm3 mol−1 cm−1 at 510 nm is a literature value and Ar(Fe) = 55.8 is from the Data Booklet.

        Chromophores and transitions

        Find the chromophore in a molecule, see which electronic transitions it can make, and predict whether it shows a band above 200 nm. A chromophore is the group of atoms responsible for the UV/visible absorption: a double or triple bond, a delocalised (conjugated) system, or an atom with a lone pair. λmax and ε are literature values (hexane or gas unless said); ε in dm3 mol−1 cm−1.

        Vibrations in 3D

        Watch each vibration of a small molecule, see its dipole moment change (or not), and count the IR absorptions. A vibration absorbs infrared (is IR active) only if it changes the dipole moment of the molecule. Here the dipole is computed live from partial charges and the moving atoms, so a vibration is active when the computed dipole changes. A molecule of N atoms has 3N − 5 vibrations if linear, 3N − 6 if not. Wavenumbers and bond lengths are literature values (gas phase); partial charges are illustrative model values.

          Greenhouse gases

          Switch gases on and off to see where each absorbs the infrared that the Earth's surface emits. The surface (about 288 K) emits infrared, most strongly near 10 µm (about 1000 cm−1). A gas is a greenhouse gas if it has IR-active vibrations in that range. This is a schematic, not a climate model: band positions are literature values, the block heights and the curve's height are not to scale.
