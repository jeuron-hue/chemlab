Three theories

Each theory widened the one before it. Read across the cards, then look at the nested diagram below.

Arrhenius 1887

Svante Arrhenius
Acid
produces H+(aq) ions in water
Base
produces OH−(aq) ions in water
HCl(g) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq) (in water)
NaOH(s) → Na+(aq) + OH−(aq) (in water)

Neutralisation

H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)

Limitations

  • Aqueous solutions only.
  • Cannot describe NH3(g) + HCl(g) → NH4Cl(s) as an acid-base reaction: there is no water, so no H+(aq) or OH−(aq).
  • Arrhenius pictured a base releasing OH− that it already contains; NH3 produces OH− only by taking H+ from water, which the theory cannot describe.
  • To fit ammonia into the Arrhenius picture it was written as “ammonium hydroxide”, NH4OH. No ionic compound NH4+OH− has ever been isolated: aqueous ammonia is mostly NH3 molecules hydrogen-bonded to water.

Brønsted-Lowry 1923

Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry, independently
Acid
proton donor
Base
proton acceptor
  • Every acid-base reaction is a proton (H+) transfer, so it needs both an acid and a base.
  • A conjugate acid-base pair is two species that differ by exactly one H+.
  • In water the proton is carried as H3O+ (hydronium ion); H+(aq) and H3O+(aq) are used interchangeably.
NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq)
pairs: NH4+ / NH3 and H2O / OH−
  • Works without water: NH3(g) + HCl(g) → NH4Cl(s) is a proton transfer from HCl to NH3.

Limitation

  • It needs a proton, so it cannot describe BF3 + NH3 → H3N→BF3.

Lewis 1923

Gilbert N. Lewis
Acid
electron-pair acceptor
Base
electron-pair donor
  • The product is an adduct held by a dative covalent (co-ordinate) bond.
BF3 + NH3 → H3N→BF3
9476 named example
  • The widest theory: no water and no proton are needed.
  • Every Brønsted-Lowry reaction is also a Lewis reaction: the base donates a lone pair to the proton, H+.
How the theories nest

Each theory includes everything inside it: every Arrhenius acid-base reaction is also a Brønsted-Lowry reaction, and every Brønsted-Lowry reaction is also a Lewis reaction.

    Water and levelling

    Water can donate or accept a proton. Its ability to accept a proton (its basicity) is what levels all the strong acids to H3O+, hiding the differences between them.

    Part A · Water is amphoteric

    Amphoteric: able to act as both an acid and a base. In Brønsted-Lowry terms, water can donate a proton or accept one.

    Bases are levelled too

    In water, each of these bases reacts completely to give OH−. They are all levelled to OH−, the strongest base that can exist in water.

    NH2− + H2O → NH3 + OH−
    O2− + H2O → 2OH−
    C2H5O− + H2O → C2H5OH + OH−
    In industry: weak bases, for example amine drug substances, are titrated with perchloric acid in glacial ethanoic acid. There HClO4 forms CH3COOH2+, a stronger acid than H3O+, which gives a sharper end-point than a titration in water.
