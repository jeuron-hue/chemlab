Explorer · 9813 1.4(b)(v)

Splitting trees

See why n equivalent neighbouring protons split a signal into n + 1 lines. Each neighbour is either aligned with the applied field (adding a tiny field) or opposed to it (subtracting one); every split halves each line, and the combinations with the same total spin land on the same line, so the line heights follow Pascal's triangle. Turn on the Enrichment switch to set the coupling constants in Hz and see what happens when two sets of neighbours couple by different amounts.

Equivalent protons

Tap the hydrogen atoms of a molecule to find its sets of equivalent protons; each set gives one signal. The lab groups the protons by symmetry, computed from the molecule's bonding (atoms with identical surroundings at every distance), and checks the grouping with the "replace by X" test: if replacing either of two hydrogens by X gives the same compound, they are equivalent.

Explorer · 9813 1.4(b)(ii), (d)(i)–(iii)

Chemical shift explorer

Why protons in different environments absorb at different δ: electronegative neighbours pull electron density away (deshielding, higher δ), π electrons set up their own field that deshields some regions and shields others (anisotropy), and hydrogen bonding deshields O–H protons. Finish with a D₂O shake, which removes the signals of labile O–H protons. Shifts are literature values in CDCl₃ and electronegativities are Pauling literature values; the ranges are from the Data Booklet.
